Every nutrition label on Earth is governed by a fundamental set of biochemical constants known as the **Atwater General Factor System**:

* **Protein:** **4 calories per gram** (4 kcal/g)
* **Carbohydrates:** **4 calories per gram** (4 kcal/g)
* **Fats:** **9 calories per gram** (9 kcal/g)
* **Alcohol:** **7 calories per gram** (7 kcal/g)

Developed in the late 19th century by pioneering American chemist Dr. Wilbur Olin Atwater using bomb calorimetry and human fecal-urinary energy balance trials, these four numbers form the backbone of modern dietetics, macro tracking, and exercise energy balance.

## The Biochemical Foundation of the Atwater System

In a closed laboratory bomb calorimeter, burning pure macronutrients releases heat energy measured in kilocalories:
* **Raw Protein:** 5.65 kcal/g
* **Raw Carbohydrates:** 4.10 kcal/g
* **Raw Fat:** 9.45 kcal/g

Why then do nutrition labels list **4, 4, and 9** instead of 5.65, 4.10, and 9.45?

Dr. Atwater realized that the human digestive tract is not an industrial furnace. Two critical biological corrections must be applied:
1. **Coefficient of Digestibility:** Humans absorb approximately 92% of protein, 97% of carbohydrates, and 95% of fats from whole foods.
2. **Incomplete Protein Oxidation (Urea Nitrogen Loss):** Unlike carbohydrates and fats, which oxidize completely into water ($H_2O$) and carbon dioxide ($CO_2$), protein contains nitrogen amino groups. The liver cannot fully oxidize nitrogen; it packages it into **urea**, which is excreted in urine, forfeiting roughly 1.25 kcal per gram of protein.

Applying these physiological corrections yields the standard **4-4-9** values:
$$\text{Protein: } (5.65 - 1.25) \times 0.92 \approx 4.05 \rightarrow \mathbf{4\text{ kcal/g}}$$
$$\text{Carbohydrates: } 4.10 \times 0.97 \approx 3.98 \rightarrow \mathbf{4\text{ kcal/g}}$$
$$\text{Fats: } 9.45 \times 0.95 \approx 8.98 \rightarrow \mathbf{9\text{ kcal/g}}$$

## The Thermic Effect of Food (TEF): The Metabolic Secret

A calorie in your mouth is not identical to a calorie in your bloodstream. The **Thermic Effect of Food (TEF)** represents the metabolic energy required by your digestive tract, pancreas, and liver to chew, secrete enzymes, transport, and biochemically assimilate nutrients:

| Macronutrient | Gross Atwater Value | Thermic Effect of Food (TEF) | Net Metabolizable Energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Protein** | 4 kcal/g | **20% – 30%** | **~2.8 – 3.2 kcal/g** |
| **Carbohydrates** | 4 kcal/g | **5% – 10%** | **~3.6 – 3.8 kcal/g** |
| **Dietary Fat** | 9 kcal/g | **0% – 3%** | **~8.7 – 9.0 kcal/g** |
| **Alcohol** | 7 kcal/g | **10% – 15%** | **~6.0 – 6.3 kcal/g** |

Notice that **protein requires roughly 25% of its own caloric value** just to break peptide bonds and synthesize new cellular proteins. If you consume 400 calories of lean chicken breast, your body expends approximately 100 calories processing it, leaving a net gain of only 300 calories.

Conversely, dietary fats are chemically almost identical to human adipose tissue. Storing dietary fat as body fat requires less than 3% energy overhead.

## How Macronutrients Fuel Your Walking Sessions

When you walk at a brisk 3.5 mph pace, your lower body muscle fibers (primarily slow-twitch Type I fibers) oxidize a combination of stored fats and carbohydrates:
* **Casual Walking (2.0 to 3.0 mph):** Up to **70% to 85% of ATP** is supplied by free fatty acid beta-oxidation (fat burn).
* **Brisk Aerobic Walking (3.5 to 4.2 mph):** The fuel mixture shifts to roughly **50% fats and 50% carbohydrates**.
* **High-Intensity Power Walking or Incline (4.5+ mph or 10%+ grade):** Carbohydrate glycogen becomes the predominant fuel source (60% to 80%).

## Practical Nutrition Takeaways

1. **Prioritize Protein on a Calorie Deficit:** High protein intake maximizes TEF and prevents your body from catabolizing skeletal muscle during walking fat-loss phases.
2. **Account for Dietary Fiber:** Net carb calculations subtract insoluble fiber because human alpha-amylase enzymes cannot cleave beta-glycosidic bonds.
3. **Beware of Liquid Alcohol Calories:** At 7 kcal/g, alcoholic beverages provide dense energy that temporarily pauses hepatic lipid oxidation without providing satiety.
